## Research question

Do employees who start working from home on some days move their commute
longer, and does their total weekly travel time change? (p. 101)

---

## Theoretical framework

The paper frames commuting as a trade-off between housing costs and travel
time; fewer commuting days lower the weekly cost of a longer trip (p. 103).

---

## Methods

### Design

Individual fixed-effects models on a household panel, comparing employees
before and after they start remote work (p. 104).

### Data

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Source** | Synthetic household panel, two waves (p. 104) |
| **N** | 2,400 employees, 4,800 person-waves (p. 105) |
| **Commute measure** | self-reported one-way commute time in minutes (p. 105) |

### Analytic strategy

Within-person change in one-way commute distance and weekly travel time
regressed on the start of remote work, with controls for job change and
relocation (p. 106).

---

## Key findings

### Commute distance

- One-way commute distance rises by 4.1 km after the start of remote work (p. 108)
- The effect is concentrated among employees who also relocate (p. 109)

### Weekly travel time

- Weekly travel time falls by 38 minutes on average (p. 110)

### Claim check

- The paper does not observe commute times directly; all times are
  self-reported (p. 105). It does not support claims about measured travel time.

---

## Implications

- Remote work may lengthen single trips while shortening weekly travel.
- Transport planning should not read longer trips as more travel.

---

## Limitations

- Two waves only (p. 117).
- Self-reported commute times (p. 117).
- Selection into remote work is not modelled (p. 118).

---

## Abstract

Using two waves of a household panel, the authors find that employees who
start remote work travel farther per trip but less per week.
