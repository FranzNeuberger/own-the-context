## Research question

How far do self-reported commute times deviate from times recorded by a
travel diary, and does the deviation depend on commute length? (p. 1)

---

## Theoretical framework

Survey-response models of retrieval and estimation: long, irregular trips are
estimated rather than recalled (p. 3).

---

## Methods

### Design

Validation study nested in one wave of a household panel: survey answers
compared with a seven-day travel diary for the same respondents (p. 4).

### Data

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Source** | Synthetic panel wave plus diary subsample (p. 4) |
| **N** | 610 respondents with both measures (p. 5) |

### Analytic strategy

Difference between reported and diary time regressed on diary time
(p. 6). Estimating equation: d_i = a + b * t_i + e_i, where d_i is the
reported minus diary time and t_i the diary time (p. 6).

---

## Key findings

- Reports are close to diary times for commutes under 30 minutes (p. 8)
- For commutes over 45 minutes, reports exceed diary times by 9 minutes on average (p. 9)
- The slope b is positive and significant, b = 0.21, p < .01 (p. 9)

---

## Implications

- Studies that compare short and long commutes by self-report overstate the gap.
- Validation subsamples are cheap relative to the bias they reveal.

---

## Limitations

- One wave only; change over time is not validated (p. 15).
- Diary completion may itself be selective (p. 15).

---

## Abstract

Self-reported commute times are accurate for short trips but inflated for
long ones.
